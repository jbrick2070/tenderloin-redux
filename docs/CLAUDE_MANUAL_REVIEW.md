## 1. Six strongest gameplay ideas, ranked

Ranking criteria: enjoyment, distinctiveness, fit with the creator's values, and cost for a first playable.

### 1. Two Visions, One Block: the layout choice belongs to neighbors
**What the player does:** Bea and Dez each pitch a layout for the closed block. **The Long Table** is Bea's: one long table, music low, built for talking. **The Open Floor** belongs to Dez and Celeste: performers facing the street, the mural as backdrop, a chalk zone, room to dance. The player sees each pitch as a translucent ghost layout and picks one. Then they place 6–8 pieces (tables, chairs, riser, speakers, cable covers, cooler) on a snap grid inside that template. A **route ribbon** runs from the curb ramp to the seating and past every doorway. It's green where the path is clear and amber where it pinches. The gathering can't open while the ribbon is amber. That is Bea's one hard rule, delivered with jokes, not penalties.

**Why it's fun:** It's a spatial puzzle with instant feedback (the Townscaper or Unpacking pleasure of things clicking into place). It sits on top of a choice that is really about people. Both neighbors are sincere, both previews look good, and the player can't fully give both what they want *this time*.

**What visibly changes:** Seating shape, where the performers stand, where the mural hangs, who gives the toast, whether Dez's beat joins the jazz, whether passersby drift in or neighbors linger, and a recorded **promise** to the neighbor whose idea waited ("Next time the speakers face the street"). The promise carries into later sessions. Fixed objects never move: Mr. Thanh's doorway, the curb ramp and Rook's cart. Rook moves the cart himself after the choice, to a spot he picks.

**First playable:** Yes. This is the bounded choice already in CONCEPT.md §4, with two neighbors and a memory attached so it doesn't read as a furniture preset.

### 2. The Layered Block Score: the music is the progress bar
**What the player does:** Nothing extra. That's the point. All four original stems loop in sync from the start, at zero volume. Each shared milestone brings one in. Choosing a layout brings in **bass** (the foundation). Sweeping brings in **brushes**, and the swish lines up with the broom strokes, so the instrument sounds like the task. Finishing the mural brings in **piano**. Soundcheck moves the trio from "practicing in the background" to a live room mix. When the block opens, Mr. Thanh steps out of his store with a muted trumpet he hasn't played in years, and the **trumpet** enters last. With the Open Floor layout, Dez's original hip-hop beat joins under the trio. That covers the creator's "optional hip-hop rhythms."

**Soundcheck itself:** Rook runs the board. The player places two speakers and sees coverage as soft color washes on the grid, trying to cover the seating while keeping Mr. Thanh's upstairs window in the quiet zone. Each trio member plays a phrase, and the player sets one fader per instrument until the meter needle sits in the band and the instrument's icon "breathes." Then a hum appears that the player can't fix. Rook finds it (a ground loop) and fixes it. A neighbor solves what the player couldn't.

**Why it's fun:** You hear your work. Every contribution is audible and the payoff accumulates, which is the emotional thesis of the whole game in under fifteen minutes.

**What visibly changes:** Musicians appear and move to their positions. String lights come on as stems enter. Captions mark each change ("[brushes enter, soft swing]") and a small stem indicator shows what's playing, so the task never depends on hearing it.

**First playable:** Yes, with synthesized placeholder stems at first (CONCEPT.md §7 already allows this). It's a core-audio feature, never part of the experimental module.

### 3. Shape-Swap Mural: neighbors bring the shapes, the player composes
**What the player does:** Celeste has primed a row of plywood panels on a fictional renovation fence. The owner's permission note is taped to it and readable. Under a fictional tag on that fence is the "actual vandalism" being covered, as an authored gameplay surface. Each neighbor hands over one original motif they drew: Bea a teacup, Dez a sneaker, Lulu a cat, Rook a fader knob, Celeste a star in her palette. Mr. Thanh hands over a trumpet bell and won't explain. That's foreshadowing. The player places, rotates and scales the motifs on a panel grid, picks one of three palettes, and builds a lettering band. They choose a short phrase from options neighbors suggested, plus a letter style Celeste teaches: block, bubble, or a simplified wildstyle. That gives the requested hip-hop-influenced lettering. Guardrails such as snapping, palette-locked colors and auto-outlining make it hard to produce something ugly.

**Why it's fun:** Creative expression with a guaranteed good result (Concrete Genie is the right reference). Neighbors react to where their piece ended up: "You put my cat on top. Correct."

**What visibly changes:** The panels appear in the finale, along the table or behind the stage depending on the layout. In the epilogue they hang in Bea's building lobby, permanently, in the save. One existing mural on the block (fictional for the first slice) can be examined but never edited, which models "preserve existing community art."

**First playable:** Yes, small: 6 motifs, 3 palettes, 1 lettering band, about 2½ minutes. Free painting tools come later.

### 4. The Group Photo, with consent
**What the player does:** After the trumpet tune, Bea calls "Picture!" The player drags neighbors into the frame. Each has a preference ("Next to Celeste. We're fighting, but we photograph well"). Rook declines to be in it and offers to take it instead. The camera then becomes his framing, and he may insist the cart gets in the corner "because it worked harder than you." The player can't override anyone's refusal.

**Why it's fun:** It's a satisfying, personal ending image. It also quietly models consent in a neighborhood that outsiders photograph without asking, with no lecture attached.

**What visibly changes:** The photo shows the chosen layout, the mural, and whoever took part. It becomes the journal's front page and the title-screen background, so the save has a face. Store the arrangement data and re-render it rather than storing pixels, which keeps saves tiny.

**First playable:** Yes. It's cheap and it's the ending beat.

### 5. Borrow and Lend, and the thank-you lap
**What the player does:** Supplies aren't fetched from nowhere. They're **borrowed**, each with a personality-shaped condition. Bea's building lends folding chairs from its community room, and Dez carries half. Mr. Thanh lends an extension cord and a cooler, provided his doorway stays clear and the cord comes back coiled over-under. Lulu lends chalk for one joke per stick. A hand-drawn **ledger** shows lines from each neighbor to their item. In the epilogue the player returns everything. Coiling the cord is a tiny tactile rhythm action (alternate directions with keys or mouse), and Rook teaches it before Mr. Thanh checks it, so the two cable perfectionists bond.

**Why it's fun:** It replaces fetch quests with a visible web of trust. The return lap gives each neighbor one more line that reflects what happened, which works as a soft, personal credits roll.

**What visibly changes:** Items physically move to the event and back. Ledger lines turn from "lent" to "returned" with a doodle.

**First playable:** Yes, three loans only. A fuller favor network comes later.
**Guardrail:** Loans are event supplies, never basic needs. The snack table is open from the first second for everyone, Rook included, and nothing about food, care or access depends on player tasks.

### 6. Ask About It, with a Real/Fictional lens
**What the player does:** Some details invite a look: the trans-pride crosswalk, the Compton's plaque, the honorary street signs (see section 6 for status), an existing mural. "Ask about it" gets each neighbor's *personal* angle, which is opinion and memory, never invented history. It also opens a **dated, sourced journal card** that carries the facts. A one-key **lens** overlays the scene, marking verified real features with a source date and fictional elements (characters, the store, the fence, the event) with a pencil icon. At the gathering, the toast-giver asks the player for an opening line and offers the stories they've discovered. Nothing is lost if they found none.

**Why it's fun:** Discovery gets *used*, not quizzed. The lens is an honesty feature that also scratches the "what's real here?" curiosity players already have about a real place.

**What visibly changes:** The toast text. A "shared at the gathering" stamp on the card. Journal growth.

**First playable:** 2–3 discoveries plus the lens. More cards, the Black Hawk lead (pointing toward Turk and Hyde) and festival history come later.

### Later-concept ideas, briefly
- **Background Day (movie shoot):** A fictional production posts "No Parking — Filming" signs. The player helps reroute a later gathering around the shoot, neighbors get paid as background extras, and the practical rain towers need a water route that doesn't cross the accessible path. It's spectacle and logistics, with no villain.
- **Agenda Builder (tenant meeting):** Fictional tenants' concerns arrive as cards. The player helps sequence an agenda, keeps everyone heard, and sets up interpretation. Disagreement is allowed to stay unresolved. All positions are fictional characters'; no real organization's stance is invented.
- **Corner dominoes table:** A neighbor teaches the game. Winning is optional, and playing unlocks a conversation and a seat.
- **Bilingual flyer:** Lettering a two-language event flyer with a neighbor. Verify the local language communities before choosing which languages.
- **Block memory:** Between sessions, neighbors' own projects move forward without the player.

**Cheap seeds for the larger concept, placed in the first playable:** a fictional tenants'-meeting flyer on a pole, a fictional filming notice, a festival poster slot that stays blank until a verified real festival is chosen, and a journal lead toward Turk and Hyde. They cost minutes, and they show the bigger ambitions are still intended.

## 2. The three largest weaknesses and the smallest practical fixes

### Weakness A: The anchor corner is more contested and more recently changed than the concept records
**Factual findings (checked today; sources in section 6):**
- Reporting describes the building at 101–121 Taylor (sources variously say 101 and 111 Taylor) as a present-day reentry and work-furlough facility run by a GEO Group subsidiary. A coalition has publicly contested that use, and in 2025 the Board of Appeals heard and reportedly rejected an appeal over it.
- The city landmarked the intersection and the lower exterior walls in 2022, and the property was added to the National Register in January 2025. An effort to expand the landmark designation to the whole building was reported but not confirmed as final.
- The 2006 sidewalk plaque was reportedly damaged during a streetscape project and was due to be replaced. I couldn't confirm that it has been.
- Taylor between Turk and Ellis was rebuilt with widened sidewalks and bulb-outs. Turk carries a westbound protected bikeway. Older map data and imagery may predate either change.
- Trans-pride crosswalks at Turk and Taylor were reported in June 2026.
- Coverage from 15 October 2026 says the game the creator is responding to used real streets around Jones and Eddy, two blocks away.

**Design judgment:** The concept treats Turk and Taylor as a cultural anchor. It's a good one, but CONCEPT.md doesn't mention the building's present use or the live dispute around it. Staging a festive fictional event right at that entrance could read as taking a side, or as ignoring the people who live there. The site's recent physical changes also make "accurate" a moving target.

**Smallest fix:**
1. Put the gathering on a **fictional temporary closure of one adjacent block face**. Choose it after the walkthrough the concept already requires, and keep it clear of the facility entrance and the plaque.
2. Make the corner an **interpretive stop**: crosswalk, plaque status and honorary signs, each with a dated journal card.
3. Draw the building's façade from current reference with no invented signage. Write a present-use card only if it's sourced and dated, and take no in-game position in the first slice.
4. Add a register category for **fictional businesses on real parcels**. Mr. Thanh's store sits on real massing with fictional signage and is labeled fictional in the lens.

Political material about the site, if any, belongs to the later political-events system and to fictional characters, consistent with CONCEPT.md §6.

### Weakness B: Five interaction modes in about fifteen minutes, with no shared verb
**Factual finding:** CONCEPT.md §4 asks the first slice to teach cleanup, space arrangement, mural composition, soundcheck, the gathering itself, and a free-roam epilogue.

**Design judgment:** That many separate mini-games means the player spends the session learning controls instead of caring about people. Each mode would end up shallow, and implementation cost multiplies.

**Smallest fix:** Build **one placement grid** and **one meter widget**, and reuse them:
- The grid places furniture (layout), motifs (mural) and speakers (soundcheck).
- The meter shows the route ribbon state, speaker coverage and fader levels.
- Cleanup reuses the same pick-and-drop to sort authored litter into three bins, if the bin colors check out.
- The Layered Block Score is the shared progress feedback.
- After the layout choice, the three middle tasks (sweep, mural, soundcheck) can be done in **any order**. Each stem must work alone or with any others, which is a composition requirement to hand the composer early.

### Weakness C: Neighbor agency and the choice's meaning are asserted, not built into the mechanics
**Factual finding:** CONCEPT.md §3–4 require neighbors with agency who can decline or redirect help, visible neighbor work before and during the player's involvement, and a choice whose consequences players notice. The concept doesn't yet say how. That's expected at this stage, but it's the riskiest gap.

**Design judgment:** The default failure in adventure games is neighbors who stand still and hand out tasks. If that happens here it contradicts the game's thesis. A "conversation vs. art-and-music" layout preset is likely to feel cosmetic unless a person and a memory are attached to it.

**Smallest fix:**
- Tie the choice to **two named neighbors' visions plus a promise** (idea 1).
- Give each neighbor a **three-state parallel task** that visibly progresses as the player completes beats: Celeste outlines and then fills panels, Rook runs cable and then tests it, Lulu chalks the zone, Bea tapes the route. Authored state machines, not simulation.
- Give each neighbor **one decline-or-redirect** line ("I've got this. Dez is drowning over there, though").
- Include **one problem only a neighbor can solve** (Rook's hum).
- **Prototype check:** after play, ask "What did your choice change?" Pass if most testers name at least two consequences without prompting.

**Smaller notes:**
- **Rook:** guard against tokenism by keeping his expertise and humor central. Housing insecurity comes up respectfully. He mentions a Monday appointment while coiling cable, and Bea offers to go with him. That's mutual support between neighbors; the player doesn't save him, and the game doesn't solve his housing.
- **The 60th anniversary:** 2026 is the 60th anniversary of the 1966 Compton's Cafeteria riot. The fictional gathering shouldn't be framed as any real commemoration (section 6).
- **The opponent game's title** differs between sources (section 6).

## 4. Implementation outline and the optional-module boundary

This is a compact plan, not an engine design. It assumes CONCEPT.md §8: browser, single player, top-down 2D, no backend.

```
src/
  core/
    world/      projected map → walkable tile layer, collision, route ribbon (grid flood-fill with clearance)
    content/    loads flat JSON: register (places/history), characters, dialogue, projects
    state/      GameState (plain data), event reducer, save envelope + migrations
    play/       PlacementGrid + Meter (shared widgets); scenes: hub, layout, sweep, mural, soundcheck, finale, photo, epilogue
    audio/      core bus: synced stem player (all stems start together at gain 0; events ramp gains), SFX bus, captions
    ui/         dialogue, journal (core tabs), ledger, lens, settings, transcripts
    modules/    ModuleHost: owns enable/disable, disposers, module audio buses, module save slices
  modules/
    neighborhood-voices/   index.ts (dynamic import only), board UI, its own data under /modules/neighborhood-voices/
content/
  register.json   id, kind (real-geo | sourced-history | fictional-character | fictional-surface | fictional-business),
                  date (present/historical), location, source, summary (original prose), verifiedOn, mediaPermission
ASSETS.csv        path, creator, method, license, rights notes, date  (CI fails on any untracked file)
```

**Save envelope** (saves happen only at core checkpoints; a module can never own a checkpoint):
```json
{
  "format": "tenderloin-redux-save", "version": 1,
  "core": {
    "checkpoint": "hub.after_choice",
    "choice": "longTable",
    "projects": { "sweep": "done", "mural": { "status": "done", "placements": [] }, "soundcheck": "todo" },
    "ledger": { "chairs": "lent", "cord": "lent", "chalk": "returned" },
    "journal": ["hist.comptons-1966", "place.turk-taylor-crosswalk"],
    "promises": ["promise.dez.speakers-face-street"],
    "photo": { "arrangement": [] }
  },
  "modules": { "neighborhoodVoices": { "v": 1, "data": {} } }
}
```
The core never reads `modules.*`. When the flag is off, the loader keeps the module slice as an **opaque blob, unparsed**, and writes it back unchanged. Re-enabling restores it, and a "Clear experimental data" button in Settings deletes it. This is the creator's call. Preserving the slice is my recommendation because it makes off and on reversible.

**Module contract:** the host owns teardown, so a forgetful module can't leak.
```ts
interface OptionalModule { id: 'neighborhoodVoices'; enable(ctx: ModuleContext): Promise<void>; }
interface ModuleContext {
  signal: AbortSignal;                                    // aborted on disable; all fetches must use it
  addJournalTab(def): Disposer;  addMapLayer(def): Disposer;  addHotspot(def): Disposer;
  addDialogueExtension(point: ExtensionPointId, nodes): Disposer;  // append-only, at named points
  audio: { createBus(): ModuleBus };                       // host-created GainNode → master; host tears it down
  state: { read(): unknown; write(v: unknown): void };     // only modules.neighborhoodVoices
}
```
`ModuleHost.disable(id)` runs in order: persist the flag, abort the signal, ramp the module bus to 0 over ≤50 ms, stop and disconnect its nodes, run every disposer in last-in-first-out order, drop the module reference, then call `core.ensureSafeScene()` so a player who was in the board UI is returned to the world.

**Where the boundary sits:**
- **The module may add:** a journal tab, a map layer of organization pins in their true locations (including outside the walkable bounds), module-owned hotspots such as the corkboard, which doesn't exist at all when the module is off, and dialogue options appended at named extension points (for example `ext.bea.after_borrow`).
- **The module may not:** edit core nodes, set core flags, affect completion, or use `new Audio()`, `AudioContext.suspend()` or the master gain.
- **Lint enforces it:** `core/**` cannot import `modules/**`, and the module cannot touch audio outside its context.

**The flag:** one setting, `experimental.neighborhoodVoices`, false by default, stored in settings rather than in the save. The build constant default is also false.

**First experiment content:** the text-only board from CONCEPT.md §5, with at most three entries. Each has an official link, a verification date and a "not a current emergency-services directory" banner. Neighbor lines point to the board ("they post their own hours, check their site") and never describe services or availability.

**Order of work (smallest useful steps):**
1. **Ground truth:** a site walk and current imagery for the chosen bounds, plus about 10 register entries.
2. **Greybox hub:** movement, talk, the placement grid, the route ribbon, the choice, synthesized stems, save and load. Playtest whether the choice reads with about 5 people before any art.
3. **Remaining scenes:** mural, soundcheck, photo, epilogue, captions, reduced motion.
4. **Module:** `ModuleHost` and the Neighborhood Voices board behind the flag, with the toggle tests from section 5 in CI.
5. **Art and audio pass:** with a provenance row per asset.

**Decisions that need a prototype, not a spec**

| Decision | Smallest test | Pass signal |
|---|---|---|
| Does the two-visions choice feel meaningful? | Greybox both layouts with placeholder dialogue | Most testers name ≥2 consequences unprompted |
| Scale compression: real block lengths vs. walk time | Greybox the bounds at 1:1, then compressed | A full loop takes under ~60 s and the topology still matches reference |
| Snap grid vs. free placement; grid cell size | Layout scene with both | Players fix an amber ribbon without help |
| Mural guardrails | 6 motifs × 3 palettes | Novices like their result in ≤2½ min |
| Stem sync and autoplay unlock | WebAudio spike on Chrome, Firefox and Safari | No drift over 15 min; audio unlocks on first input |
| Renderer (Canvas 2D vs. a small library) | Map plus 20 sprites plus the placement UI | Holds 60 fps on a mid laptop; code stays small |
| Dialogue format (plain JSON vs. a library such as Ink) | Write Bea's scene in both | Pick the one the writer prefers; check the license |
| Soundcheck readable without sound | Muted playtest | Completed using only meters and captions |
| Walkable-layer authoring tool | Author one block | Doorways and crossings match reference photos |

## 5. Checks that prove the experiment can be turned off

**Static checks (CI):**
1. A lint rule fails if `core/**` imports `modules/**`, or if the module calls `new Audio`, `AudioContext.suspend` or touches the master gain directly.
2. A bundle check: the module is a separate chunk, and a canary string from the module (for example `NV_CANARY_7f3a`) does **not** appear in the main chunk.
3. A provenance check: every file under `public/` and `assets/` has a row in `ASSETS.csv` with a license. No row may reference original TenderVoice material unless a verified source-and-license record exists. This holds **regardless of the flag**, because a disabled switch doesn't make an asset acceptable to bundle.

**Automated runtime checks** (a browser test harness, plus a debug autopilot that plays the core path):

4. **Off from a fresh profile:** the autopilot completes the gathering. The network log has zero requests under `/modules/neighborhood-voices/`. The DOM has no `[data-module="nv"]`. `ModuleHost.disposerCount('nv') === 0`, and no module audio bus exists.
5. **Enable mid-session:** the board tab, its pins and its hotspot appear, and the data fetch happens now, not before.
6. **Disable while module audio plays.** The first experiment is text-only, so run this against a **test-only fixture build** with a synthetic tone clip. That proves teardown before any real audio exists.
